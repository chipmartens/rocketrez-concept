# Why the new site works the way it does

The reasoning behind the RocketRez spec site, decision by decision, with the evidence for each one. Written for the RocketRez team, November 2026. Nothing here is a claim about RocketRez's results: every number on the site traces to rocketrez.com, a named customer, or is labelled as sample data.

## 1. The one idea

The site is built on one sentence: RocketRez runs tickets, food, retail, memberships and guest texting on one system, so every guest's spend adds up to one number an operator can grow. That number is per-cap, spend per guest. The homepage says it as "Sell the whole day, not just the ticket."

We got there the way a positioning exercise should: start with what a buyer would do if RocketRez didn't exist, then find what only RocketRez does, then the value of that, then who cares most.

- **What buyers do instead.** The real competitor is the stitched stack: a booking widget, a gate till, a café till, a shop system and a membership spreadsheet. Operators say so in their own words. Hemker Park and Zoo: "we had two different systems here ... it was just creating chaos between our guests and our staff." A 2024 reviewer: "We were using 3 programs to do the job of just RRZ!" Maui Ocean Center's GM: "in the past, it's been 'garbage in, garbage out'." That makes doing nothing the competitor to beat, more than any rival vendor (decision 3 below).
- **What only RocketRez does.** One inventory across web, gate, travel sites, café, shop, photo and members, with Relay guest texting inside the ticketing system, built and supported by people who have run attractions.
- **Why it matters.** When every sale lands in one place, an operator can finally see one guest's whole day and grow it. Per-cap is the score operators already keep. RocketRez's own site already used the phrase "operators who treat per-cap like a scoreboard", and no competitor names per-cap in a headline (research/competitors.md). It's a fight RocketRez can own.
- **Who cares most.** Independent zoos, aquariums, waterparks, tours, museums and towers that earn at more than the gate and staff up with seasonal crews.

"The revenue platform for attractions" stays as the category line in the page title and footer. It frames RocketRez against ticketing tools on a dimension they don't measure. The headline does the selling.

## 2. Decision by decision

**1. Per-cap is the number the whole site points at.**
- What we did: one owned metric, glossed as "spend per guest" on first use, carried by an amber counter in the hero and footer.
- Why: buyers already measure themselves this way, and a single number is easier to remember than eighteen modules.
- Proof: our method is to stand apart rather than out-feature rivals, and no competitor names per-cap in a headline (research/competitors.md). One recognisable asset, used consistently, is what makes a brand retrievable when a buyer is choosing (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, *How Brands Grow*; *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*).

**2. The hero is one guest's day.**
- What we did: a day drawn from 9:00 to 17:00. Ticket, lunch, photo, plush and membership upgrade drop onto it and the per-cap counter adds them up.
- Why: a GM should understand the product in five seconds, before reading a word of body copy.
- Proof: about 57% of viewing time sits above the fold and 74% within the first two screens, so the value statement belongs in screen one (Nielsen Norman Group, "Scrolling and attention"). A visitor should be able to say what you sell, to whom, and how it differs from the first screen (NN/g B2B website checklist).

**3. Five systems collapse into one record.**
- What we did: a till receipt, a café ticket, a booking email and a membership spreadsheet showing "#N/A", each with its own total, merge into one guest record.
- Why: it shows the buyer their own Monday morning. It argues against doing nothing, which is where most deals die.
- Proof: our method treats doing nothing as the main competitor, so the cost of the status quo has to be visible. When people move data between systems by hand, a single access point is the fix (NN/g, complex application design). Buyers name the same triggers: two systems "creating chaos" (Hemker Park and Zoo) and reports pulled from "two or three separate reports" and hand-edited in Excel (a museum on the AAM Open Forum, 2025).

**4. Every product view is a working recreation of a real RocketRez screen, with sample data.**
- What we did: timed-entry calendars, the POS, member cards, Relay threads and Insights, rebuilt in HTML from real screens. Clickable, labelled "Sample data", no raster screenshots.
- Why: buyers want to see the product before they book a call. Static screenshots age, blur and can't be explored.
- Proof: let hesitant buyers see the product without IT approval (NN/g, B2B usability). Let people learn by doing, with results shown right away (NN/g, complex application design). Customer.io, the design reference, uses only real UI, cropped to one component (reference-study/customerio/study.md).

**5. One overlay per screen, and every product card has a benefit headline.**
- What we did: each screen gets one callout designed for that screen, plus a headline and one or two sentences that say what it does for the operator.
- Why: a screen with five callouts says nothing. A caption like "POS" makes the reader do the translation.
- Proof: the one item that differs is the one remembered, and more than one cancels out (Von Restorff effect, Laws of UX). Put the main point first and the detail after it (NN/g, inverted pyramid), so each card leads with what it does for the operator and lets the screen prove it.

**6. The platform is organised by jobs: Sell, Serve, Keep, Know, plus Relay.**
- What we did: the live site's 18 module pages fold into four jobs an operator recognises, with Relay as its own fifth item.
- Why: operators think "sell more before they arrive" and "bring them back", not "Upsells and Cross Sells".
- Proof: use the user's words, not internal category names (NN/g heuristic 2). Decision time grows with the number of options (Hick's law). Groups need a name the reader understands, or they're just a smaller pile (chunking, Laws of UX).

**7. Industry pages name each industry's problems.**
- What we did: each venue page opens with what that operator fights, in their terms, and how RocketRez answers it. Tours get their own headline: "Fill every departure, from every channel."
- Why: a zoo's problem (members at every till) is not a boat tour's (tickets that only sell at the dock). A 75-minute cruise has no "whole day", so tours get a different lead.
- Proof: buyers pull brands from memory through buying situations; link the brand to one situation per page (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, Category Entry Points). Segment by how customers describe themselves (NN/g B2B navigation). Buyer quotes: "The ability to manage memberships has been a vexing process" (Mennonite Heritage Village); "The only option was to purchase tickets just as you were getting on the boat" (Governors Island).

**8. Customer identity is real.**
- What we did: real customer logos only (eight files on hand), real venue photos, quotes with a name and title, results with a date. Fossil Rim's "65% more business with 55% less staffing" always carries "since its May 2020 launch".
- Why: a typed name in a logo row reads as filler. An undated result invites a CFO to discount it.
- Proof: testimonials earn trust with the person's title and company (NN/g B2B proof). Unknown brands need a lot of reassurance, and concrete specifics like named customers beat generic claims (Baymard Institute, trust and credibility). Every number names its customer (Customer.io study, writing rule 8).

**9. Comparison pages say where a rival is stronger.**
- What we did: pages for ROLLER, the tour booking tools, the legacy suites and the stitched stack. Sources dated, "Not stated" where the public record is silent, and a "Where they're a good fit" column.
- Why: buyers compare anyway. If RocketRez doesn't write the comparison, a competitor does, and AI answers quote it.
- Proof: balanced testimonials beat glowing ones (NN/g). Give buyers evaluation criteria that favour your strengths, honestly (NN/g B2B). RocketRez appeared in none of six AI answers to buyer questions tested, partly because only competitors had written comparison pages (strategy/SEO-AEO.md).

**10. Pricing is a "build your quote" object, and every fee is shown.**
- What we did: two choices, about a minute, producing the layout of a real quote with every fee line in it, plus the contract terms to get in writing.
- Why: buyers in this category have been burned. Reviews of other vendors: "They increased an already high price by 7%"; "extraction is basically a blackmail."
- Proof: price is the information B2B visitors rank highest, and hiding it reads as evasive (NN/g, "Show price"). Reassure buyers there will be no surprise costs, and cover switching and exit terms on the pricing page (NN/g B2B pricing). Honest note: NN/g also says a starting price or a range beats a calculator. The site is ready for one the day RocketRez publishes it.

**11. Security is split into "Published" and "Sent on request".**
- What we did: what rocketrez.com already states (PCI-compliant processing through Stripe, Shift4, PayPal or Sola; permissions; audit logs) on one side, and what an IT review asks for, offered on request, on the other.
- Why: the buyer red team's museum IT manager could not finish a security questionnaire from the site and marked RocketRez a risk.
- Proof: integration and compliance must be explicit; it is the page a skeptical IT reviewer reads (NN/g B2B specs). Never fake a badge for a certification you do not hold (Baymard).

**12. Switching gets a go-live calculator and a board outline.**
- What we did: enter an opening day, get a start date with the maths shown (from RocketRez's published 8 to 12 weeks). A printable "For your board" outline. A page of RFP questions to send every vendor.
- Why: the off-season is the real deadline, and non-profit directors need something to hand their board.
- Proof: your champion has to sell for you internally; give them material they can paste into their own documents (NN/g B2B). Budgets are approved by other people, so address switching costs, migration help and exit terms where buyers decide (NN/g B2B pricing).

**13. The Relay calculator shows every assumption.**
- What we did: the operator enters ticket volume and spend per guest; each text's take rate and price is visible and editable, labelled as a starting guess, never a benchmark.
- Why: the live calculator hides its lift percentages. A CFO trusts a model they can argue with.
- Proof: persuasion uses true information; deception steers people to a choice they wouldn't make if they saw the full picture (Laws of UX ethics line, NN/g deceptive patterns). Every number needs a reference point so the reader can judge it (NN/g, dashboards).

**14. Learn is a documentation app, with search.**
- What we did: the Academy, Signal podcast, guides, blog and webinars sit in their own slim shell with search across about 270 items, and nothing basic sits behind a form.
- Why: operators come to Learn with a question, not to browse a marketing page.
- Proof: help should be searchable and task-focused (NN/g heuristic 10). About half of people prefer search to menus, so both must work (Baymard). Do not gate FAQs and how-to content; it supports the shortlist (NN/g). The gated glossary was RocketRez's best asset for AI answers (strategy/SEO-AEO.md).

**15. Plain operator voice, with banned words.**
- What we did: short, specific, a little dry. Banned: "seamless", "magical", "unforgettable", "revolutionize", "world-class", "all-in-one", "solutions", "empower".
- Why: operators praise RocketRez as "one system"; that's their phrase, so the site uses it. "All-in-one" as a vendor claim is the phrase every rival uses.
- Proof: concise, scannable, objective copy measured 124% more usable than promotional copy (NN/g, "Concise, scannable and objective"). Cut promotional language; objective, specific claims scan and convince better than hype (NN/g UX writing). Use the practitioner's terms and define them on first use (NN/g, specialised words).

**16. No kicker labels.**
- What we did: no small capitals label above headings.
- Why: the heading should say the thing.
- Proof: readers scan headings and the first two words of each line (NN/g layer-cake pattern; "First 2 words"). A label in that slot spends the reader's attention on a category, not the point.

**17. A strict type scale and spacing system.**
- What we did: marketing text in seven sizes (14 to 44 px, body 18), one size per role, spacing only from ten tokens. An audit script fails the build on anything off-scale.
- Why: an early build had 24 different font sizes, which read as "randomly sized" even when no single choice was wrong.
- Proof: about three type sizes per view; if everything is emphasised, nothing is (NN/g visual design). Visual consistency is a usability issue, and equal padding keeps groups readable (NN/g proximity). Attractive interfaces are judged easier to use (aesthetic-usability effect).

**18. Colour fields and dark sections.**
- What we did: Rez blue, night and amber fields behind product views, a dark band or two per page, white as the resting ground.
- Why: an all-white site reads as unfinished, and product cards on white disappear.
- Proof: Customer.io puts UI cards on a colour field, never on white, and uses one or two dark bands per page (reference-study/customerio/study.md). Rez blue #0051F9 is kept because a brand asset only compounds with consistent use (Ehrenberg-Bass).

**19. Accessibility: WCAG 2.2 AA, down to 390 px.**
- What we did: 4.5:1 text contrast, keyboard paths through every recreation, 24 px minimum targets, reduced motion respected, no sideways scroll at 390 px.
- Why: seasonal staff and guests use phones. Some buyers use screen readers. Public institutions often require it.
- Proof: W3C WCAG 2.2 (1.4.3 contrast, 2.5.8 target size, 2.4.11 focus not obscured). In the WebAIM Million 2026 study, 95.9% of home pages had a detectable failure, and low contrast was on 83.9%. 71.6% of screen reader users move through a long page by headings (WebAIM survey 10).

**20. One action, one name: "Book a demo".**
- What we did: the same label in the nav, every page and every closing band, with "See pricing" second. The demo form has five fields and says what happens next.
- Why: three labels for one action make people wonder if they're three actions.
- Proof: one action with different labels across a site is a classic consistency failure (NN/g heuristic 4). One primary action per view (Von Restorff). Demo forms: 3 to 5 fields, say what happens next and who replies (NN/g contact pages; Baymard lead-gen forms).

**21. Claims discipline.**
- What we did: every number traces to research/fact-base.md, a named customer, or the live site, or is labelled sample data. The Relay growth quote is attributed exactly to who said it: H Two Marketing, the agency for Wild Water Adventure Park, not the park itself.
- Why: the first thing a CFO does is check a claim. One that fails costs the whole page.
- Proof: never show a badge or credential you do not hold (Baymard). Objective, specific claims convince better than hype (NN/g UX writing).

**22. Search and AI-answer structure.**
- What we did: titles in buyer search terms ("Software for zoos and wildlife parks | RocketRez"), one H1 per page, answer-first sections. Structured data is being added in the final SEO pass.
- Why: RocketRez gets about 814 US organic visits a month; ROLLER gets 5.8 times that (Semrush, November 2026). It loses category searches, not its own name.
- Proof: exact-match pages and 40 to 60 word definitions are what answer engines lift (strategy/SEO-AEO.md). Review count and recency on Capterra and G2 feed most of those answers. Put the main point first (NN/g inverted pyramid).

## 3. What we deliberately did not do

- **No AI imagery.** RocketRez's current photos are AI-generated families at kiosks. Every photo on the new site is a real customer venue from Wikimedia Commons, credited. Our view: when anyone can generate a perfect image, a real one is what earns trust. NN/g's About research says the same: real photos of real people and places, not stock.
- **No stock or raster screenshots.** Every product view is a working recreation of a real screen (decision 4).
- **No invented numbers.** No customer count, uptime, per-cap lift or price that RocketRez hasn't published. Where one belongs, the page is built to take it.
- **No kicker labels** (decision 16).
- **No fake urgency.** No countdowns, no "only 3 spots left". Invented scarcity is a deceptive pattern (NN/g), and in our experience buyers in a risky purchase need risk taken away, not pressure added. The only deadline on the site is the real one: the operator's own opening day.

## 4. What RocketRez would need to supply to make it real

- Customer count and countries
- Tickets or dollars processed per year
- A per-cap lift across three or more customers, with the method
- Confirmation that Insights reports spend per guest by department, and that each guest has one record across every sale
- Support response median, uptime history and a status page
- Security attestations (SOC 2 status, PCI level, SSO and MFA, hosting region)
- A starting price or range, and contract terms (length, renewal cap, data export on exit)
- Confirmation of the 8 to 12 week go-live and the demo reply window
- More customer logo files, staff portraits, and permission for venue photos and quotes
- The guide PDFs behind the Learn forms
- A fresh round of reviews (the newest are from mid 2024)
